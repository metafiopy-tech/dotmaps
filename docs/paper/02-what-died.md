# What Died

A whitepaper that only publishes its wins isn't a whitepaper, it's an ad.
So here's the finding that failed, at the same size as the ones that
held, because the discipline that makes the receipt trustworthy is the
same discipline that makes it publish its own funerals.

**The claim under test:** does everything a colony already knows make
*new* learning cheaper? The instinct says yes — surely knowing more
things means fewer wasted attempts figuring out one more.

**The verdict, written before a single run and graded exactly as
written:** no. Across four separate trials and fifty-two paired runs, a
colony that already knew things was never cheaper per new fact learned
than one starting from nothing — and in the cleanest, final trial, it
was measurably *more* expensive (+16.2%, decisively outside the
pre-registered tolerance band). The efficiency claim is dead. Not
"inconclusive," not "needs more data" — dead, by the exact rule the team
wrote for itself before looking at a single result.

**What killed it, once the team went looking:** the early trials'
apparent discount wasn't efficiency at all — it was wasted effort being
avoided. Earlier runs kept re-trying claims that were already settled,
burning attempts on their own confusion. Once a mechanical gate stopped
that re-trying for every arm of the experiment equally, the "discount"
the equipped colony had was showing evaporated with it. The saving had
never been about knowing more; it had been about repeating yourself
less, and once *nobody* repeated themselves, the two conditions cost the
same.

That's a real finding, and it's a smaller, less flattering one than the
team went in hoping for. It gets kept, at full size, right next to
everything that worked — because a receipt that only shows up when the
news is good isn't a receipt.

**receipts:** `runs/e1d-verdict/verdict.json` — the frozen final-trial
clause, executed exactly as written, verbatim: "BANK'S EFFICIENCY CLAIM
DIES PERMANENTLY."
